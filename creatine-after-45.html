CREATINE AFTER 45: WHAT THE RESEARCH ACTUALLY SAYS.

Creatine may be one of the most studied supplements in sports nutrition. That doesn't mean every claim about it deserves to survive contact with the evidence.

BUILT50 • THE LAB • 2026

Creatine has been around long enough that it almost feels boring.

In fitness, that's practically a compliment.

While new supplements arrive every year with increasingly dramatic promises, creatine monohydrate has accumulated decades of research examining its effects on training performance, strength and body composition.

More recently, researchers have paid increasing attention to what it might mean for adults as they get older.

The interesting part isn't that creatine is new. It's that we're still learning where an old supplement may actually be useful.

FIRST: CREATINE ISN'T A SHORTCUT.

The first thing to get out of the way is the idea that adding creatine somehow replaces good training.

It doesn't.

Resistance training remains the main event. Creatine is better thought of as a possible adjunct — something that may improve certain outcomes when combined with the work you're already doing.

That's especially important when talking about aging because the conversation can quickly drift from reasonable evidence into "anti-aging miracle" territory.

The current evidence doesn't justify that kind of claim.

WHAT DOES THE NEWER RESEARCH SHOW?

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis examined randomized controlled trials in adults age 60 and older who combined resistance training with creatine supplementation.

Across 11 trials, researchers found that adding creatine produced a small additional improvement in muscular strength compared with resistance training alone.

That's encouraging — but the same analysis did not find clear additional benefits for muscle mass or physical function. The certainty of evidence for those outcomes was also lower.

2026 META-ANALYSIS

Strength improved. Everything else was less certain.

The analysis included 11 randomized controlled trials involving adults age 60 and older.

Creatine plus resistance training produced a small but statistically significant additional benefit for muscle strength compared with resistance training alone.

Additional effects on muscle mass and physical function were not statistically clear.

THAT ISN'T THE ONLY EVIDENCE.

Other reviews have reached somewhat more favorable conclusions, particularly when looking at lean tissue and specific strength measures.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of eight randomized trials reported improvements in lower-body strength and lean tissue mass when creatine was combined with resistance training in older adults.

Earlier meta-analyses have also reported improvements in lean tissue mass and upper- and lower-body strength.

Put together, the research is more interesting than either extreme usually presented online:

Creatine isn't useless — and it isn't magic.

Good science is usually less exciting than marketing and more useful than hype.

WHY MIGHT STRENGTH MATTER MORE AS WE AGE?

Maintaining strength becomes increasingly important as adults get older, not simply because of what happens in the gym, but because muscular strength contributes to the ability to keep doing normal life.

That's one reason the newer evidence is worth paying attention to.

A small improvement is still an improvement — particularly when it is layered on top of a well-designed resistance-training program.

But it's also why the size of the effect matters.

Saying something provides a measurable benefit is not the same as saying it transforms human aging.

WHAT ABOUT MUSCLE SIZE?

This is where the research becomes less tidy.

Some earlier analyses reported increased lean tissue mass when creatine was combined with resistance training, while the newer 2026 analysis did not find a statistically clear additional benefit for muscle mass across its included studies.

Differences between populations, training programs, study duration and research methods can all influence results.

That's why one study or one meta-analysis shouldn't automatically become a universal rule.

AND WHAT ABOUT THE BRAIN?

Creatine is also being studied outside traditional strength and muscle outcomes, including possible effects involving cognition and brain energy metabolism.

That research is interesting, but the evidence is not strong enough to turn creatine into a blanket claim about preventing cognitive decline or improving every aspect of brain function.

This is exactly where THE LAB philosophy matters: promising research deserves attention without pretending the conclusion has already been written.

The BUILT50 takeaway

  • Resistance training remains the foundation.
  • Creatine may provide an additional strength benefit in older adults.
  • Evidence for added muscle-mass benefits is less consistent.
  • Claims involving cognition and longevity should remain measured.
  • One supplement does not replace training, nutrition, sleep or recovery.

SO... IS CREATINE WORTH TALKING ABOUT?

Absolutely.

Not because it suddenly became a revolutionary anti-aging compound, but because it has a substantial research history and continues to be studied in populations that matter to BUILT50.

The evidence suggests there may be useful benefits — especially when creatine is combined with resistance training — while also leaving plenty of questions researchers are still working through.

That's a much better story than either:

"Creatine changes everything."

or

"Supplements don't matter."

The useful answer usually lives somewhere between the two.
